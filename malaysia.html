Malaysia

What foreigners can actually buy here, the visas that come attached, and what it costs on the way in and the way out. Written from Penang, a couple of weeks after we landed.

Last checked 19 September 2026

Article 1 of a series. Coming next: Penang area guide · projects I'm scouting · our own planning

Read this first. This is me sharing what I found, not legal, tax or immigration advice. Malaysian rules change often and differ by state, and some of what's below could already be out of date or simply wrong. Every figure is marked so you can see where it came from. Before you sign anything, check it with a licensed Malaysian lawyer.

Official checked on a government site Reported from law firms, agents or developers, not yet confirmed at the source

Five days in, three conversations

Five days into Penang and I'd already had three separate conversations about buying property here.

So I did what I always do. I went and checked.

Malaysia is not Indonesia. No PT PMA, no holding company in Singapore. Foreigners can own qualifying property directly, in their own name, freehold included. Sounds simple. It isn't. The real question here isn't freehold or leasehold. It's whether this specific property is open to foreigners at all.

The price gate

Each state sets a minimum price for foreign buyers. In Penang it depends on where and what: the island costs more than the mainland, a house more than an apartment.

PenangApartment / condoLanded house
IslandRM1,000,000approx €213,000RM3,000,000approx €638,000
Mainland (Seberang Perai)RM500,000approx €106,000RM1,000,000approx €213,000

Reported Consistent across most Malaysian property and legal guides. One source lists RM600,000 for mainland apartments, so check the current figure. Confirm the exact threshold for your specific property with a licensed Malaysian lawyer before you rely on either number.

Clear the price and you still need approval from the state. Lawyers quote anywhere from four to eight weeks up to several months. Reported There's also money on top of the price for that approval, and here the sources genuinely disagree with each other, not just on the number. Some describe a flat consent fee, commonly quoted around RM10,000 for residential property. Others describe a percentage state levy instead, cited anywhere from about 1% to 3% depending on island or mainland. Reported I haven't gone through it myself yet. When I do, I'll update this with what it actually was, not what the internet guessed. Until then, get the timeline and the exact fee or levy confirmed by a licensed Malaysian lawyer before you budget around either.

Some things are closed to foreigners whatever you pay: Malay reserve land, the Bumiputera quota units inside a development, low and medium-cost housing, agricultural land. Landed property is far more restricted than apartments, especially on the island. Title and tenure decide more than the price tag does.

The company workaround that isn't one

The first idea everyone has: buy through a company. I looked into it. A foreign-owned company faces the same price gate, the same state approval, the same closed units. It's the same rules with extra paperwork. Reported

What happens with tax if you later sell the shares instead of the property depends on the structure. That's a lawyer and tax adviser question, not something I'll flatten into one line. For one ordinary property, the yearly accounting and compliance costs usually outweigh the benefit. With three or more properties, a partnership or estate planning, it's a different conversation.

If you don't want to buy at all

Here's a route I hadn't considered: Malaysian REITs, listed property funds on the Kuala Lumpur exchange. No foreign ownership limit, no price gate, no state approval. You open a brokerage account and buy units. Yields have been running roughly 5 to 8% a year. Reported One thing to check before you do this: the withholding tax on distributions to non-residents went up to 30% for the 2026 tax year, replacing the old flat 10%. It's a real change, not a typo, so recalculate the yield before assuming it's the easy option. Reported LHDN Practice Note 2/2026, via several Malaysian tax advisory firms, not yet checked by me directly against the Note itself.

Not the same as a place you can walk into. But if the honest answer to "do I want to deal with all of the above" is no, this is the honest alternative.

Getting residency out of it

Malaysia's long-stay visa, MM2H, was rebuilt in 2024. Buying property used to be optional. Now it's required, and you generally can't sell it for ten years unless you upgrade to something pricier. Official

Official MM2H rows from mm2h.gov.my. All MM2H visas are renewable. If you're 25 to 49 you need to spend 90 days a year in Malaysia; 50 and over, no minimum. Reported PVIP row from licensed agents.

The special zones row is the one to look at twice. The best-known example is Forest City in Johor, a huge development across the water from Singapore that has sat half-empty for years. According to the developer's own guidance, you buy from RM500,000 (approx €106,000) directly from them, complete within 90 days and hold for ten years. You don't have to live there. Reported Cheaper door, but a single development with a long track record of struggling to fill up. I'd go and look before I believed anything.

The Premium Visa skips property completely, with full work rights and no minimum stay. The bar is income: about RM40,000 (approx €8,500) a month from outside Malaysia, plus a RM200,000 (approx €42,500) government fee. Reported Built for people already earning well abroad.

What it costs, in and out

On the way in: stamp duty for foreigners doubled on 1 January 2026, from 4% to a flat 8% of the price. On a RM1,000,000 apartment that's RM80,000 (approx €17,000). Permanent residents are excluded. Reported Multiple Malaysian law firms and KPMG's own tax note trace this to Item 32(ab) of the Stamp Act 1949, inserted by the Finance Act 2025 (Act 874), gazetted 31 December 2025. That is a specific enough citation that I believe it, but I still have not opened the gazette page myself, so it stays Reported until I do. Have your own lawyer confirm the exact rate against the Act before you sign anything, not just this page.

On the way out: sell within five years and the tax is 30% of your gain, not of the price. From year six it drops to 10%. And stays there. Malaysians eventually pay nothing. Foreigners never do. Official hasil.gov.my

So, is Malaysia open to foreign money?

Depends what kind.

If you want to buy and flip, the direction is clearly less friendly than a year ago. Higher stamp duty, a gains tax that never reaches zero, all framed by the government as protecting local affordability.

BUT.

At the same time they rebuilt MM2H, launched the Premium Visa and are pushing tax breaks for Forest City. That's not a country closing its doors. It's a country getting pickier about who walks through them. Quick money, less welcome. Money that comes with people who actually live here, actively courted.

Not sure yet how that plays out. It does make you think about the difference between a place that wants your money and a place that wants you.

What's pulling you here, the property or the visa? Because right now, Malaysia is answering those two questions differently.
